Good direction — and your instinct is basically right, but with one asterisk: standard Wordle actually has been fully solved exactly (Alex Selby's solver, ~3.421 average guesses optimal) via branch-and-bound, not raw brute force. What makes it tractable is the same trick chess/Go engines use: don't expand every branch, prune hard and search selectively. That's the throughline for everything below.

Why "solve everything" is currently out of reach here

Your setup is harder than classic Wordle: 3209 weighted solutions × 14855 guesses, 6-guess budget, weighted objective (not just worst-case or unweighted average). The game tree branches 3^5=243 ways per guess and the guess choice itself is a 15k-way branching factor — full expansion is hopeless past depth 1-2. But note: your current entropy/expected-pool-size strategies are already exact 1-ply evaluation (real bucket counts via score_matrix, not sampled) — that's actually stronger than a typical chess eval function, which is always approximate. The open opportunity is depth beyond 1-ply, which today doesn't exist at all.

Approaches worth exploring, roughly cheapest-to-most-ambitious:

1. Selective/beam expectimax (the Selby move) — take your existing entropy ranking, keep only the top-K guesses (say 20-50), and for those only recurse one more ply: actually play out each bucket and exact-evaluate the best guess in the resulting sub-pool, sum up the true expected/weighted cost. This is literally alpha-beta's "move ordering + selective depth" idea, and it's the cheapest thing on this list — no ML, just recursion + memoization + a beam width. Transposition-table the candidate pool (frozenset of words) since many histories collapse to the same pool.
2. Branch-and-bound with information-theoretic pruning — add a lower bound (⌈log₃|pool|⌉ more guesses needed) at each node and prune any branch whose best-case can't beat your current incumbent. This is what makes Selby's search finish in reasonable time despite the huge tree — pure algorithmic technique, no model needed.
3. Monte Carlo rollouts for the deeper ply — instead of exact recursion (which explodes past 2 ply), sample solutions from the weighted distribution, run your current greedy solver to completion on each sample, and average the guess count. This gives you an approximate multi-ply value cheaply — literally the "rollout" half of MCTS, without needing the tree-search half yet.
4. Full MCTS with UCB1 — treat guesses as actions, use rollouts (as above) as the leaf evaluation, and let UCB1 allocate simulation budget toward promising guesses instead of exhaustively expanding all 15k at every node. Where this actually earns its keep over pure beam search: it naturally handles "worth investing search budget here" decisions without you hand-tuning a beam width.
5. NNUE-style learned evaluator — train a small fast model (even gradient-boosted trees, doesn't need to be a net) to predict "expected remaining guesses" from summary features of a candidate pool (size, entropy, weighted mass, letter-frequency stats) instead of computing it exactly. Bootstrap training labels from cases small enough to solve exactly (pools under some size threshold), then use the model to evaluate deeper/larger nodes where exact recursion is too expensive. This is the direct parallel to NNUE replacing Stockfish's hand-crafted eval — same motivation (exact evaluation too slow at scale).
6. Policy net for guess pre-selection — a model that maps pool state → a shortlist of promising guesses, replacing your current "rank by entropy" pre-filter with something learned. This is the Leela/AlphaZero policy-head idea: use it to narrow branching factor before search, not to replace search.
7. Expert iteration / self-play (full AlphaZero loop) — alternate between (a) using search (beam/MCTS) to generate better-than-greedy training labels and (b) training the value/policy models on those labels, repeating. Most ambitious, most likely to actually beat hand-tuned heuristics on the weighted objective specifically, since nobody's hand-tuned entropy for an arbitrary frequency-weighted target distribution the way they have for uniform Wordle.

My take: start with #1+#2 (selective expectimax + pruning) — it's a few hours of work on top of infrastructure you already have (fast_scoring, analysis.analyze_all), gives you a real multi-ply improvement over greedy, and tells you empirically how much headroom is even left before greedy's 1-ply heuristics. That number should drive whether #3-7 are worth the much bigger investment.
